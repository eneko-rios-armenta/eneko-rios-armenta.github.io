Exoplanet Population Analysis

Can planetary mass and radius reveal different physical regimes among exoplanets?

01 Question

Do exoplanet mass-radius relations exhibit discrete physical boundaries?

As the catalog of confirmed exoplanets grows, understanding the transitions between terrestrial planets, sub-Neptunes, gas giants, and brown dwarfs becomes essential to planetary science.

This project investigates whether empirical mass-radius data reveal distinct power-law regimes or critical transition points that correspond to underlying physical structures (such as core composition, envelope retention, and degenerate equation-of-state behaviors).

01 Transition Regimes

Identifying statistical break points in scaling relations between super-Earths and sub-Neptunes.

02 Equation of State Limits

Testing where electron degeneracy pressure causes radius plateauing in high-mass gas giants.

02 Data

NASA Exoplanet Archive Dataset & Filtering

Data was queried from the official NASA Exoplanet Archive PS (Planetary Systems) table, consisting of thousands of confirmed exoplanets with measured physical properties.

To ensure robust statistical inference, raw observations were filtered based on measurement uncertainties and discovery methods:

  • Precision Filtering: Excluded candidates with relative mass errors $\sigma_M / M > 20\%$ or radius errors $\sigma_R / R > 10\%$.
  • Detection Method Selection: Focused primarily on planets measured via transit photometry combined with radial velocity or transit timing variations (TTVs).
  • Derived Properties: Calculated mean bulk densities ($\rho$) and equilibrium temperatures ($T_{\text{eq}}$) based on stellar host parameters.
03 Main Result

Clear power-law slope changes at key mass thresholds

Fitting piecewise power-law models ($R \propto M^\gamma$) reveals statistically significant breakpoints in the scaling index $\gamma$.

The empirical exponent transitions sharply near $\sim 5\text{ }M_\oplus$, marking the shift from rocky interiors to volatile gas envelope retention, and again near Jupiter-mass scales where interior compression dominates.

04 Robustness

Uncertainty quantification & resampling checks

To test whether these breaks are artifacts of sample selection or observational bias, we applied Monte Carlo error propagation and bootstrapping routines.

Resampling 10,000 synthetic catalogs within reported measurement error bounds confirms that the breakpoint locations remain consistent within confidence intervals.

05 Explanation

Physical mechanisms driving the transitions

The observed population structure aligns with key theoretical mechanisms in planetary formation and evolution:

In lower mass regimes, photoevaporation and core-accretion thresholds dictate atmospheric retention. At the upper end ($M > 1\text{ }M_J$), planetary radius becomes nearly independent of mass due to coulomb ionization and electron degeneracy in giant gas interiors.

06 Limitations

Selection effects & observational biases

While the results provide strong statistical trends, several limitations must be considered:

  • Detection Bias: Transit surveys preferentially discover short-period planets, biasing the sample toward highly irradiated worlds.
  • Mass Precision: Planets measured via radial velocity often carry $\sin(i)$ inclination ambiguities unless combined with transits.
  • Stellar Age Variations: Young gas giants are thermally inflated, which broadens radius scatter for planets of similar mass.
07 Conclusion

Summary & future directions

This empirical analysis demonstrates clear structural shifts in the exoplanet population, mapping directly onto underlying astrophysical phase transitions.

Future datasets from ongoing missions like TESS and upcoming facilities like PLATO will refine mass-radius precision, allowing finer mapping of composition boundaries and atmospheric retention limits.
